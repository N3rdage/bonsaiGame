## Math sub-series

A recurring sub-series on the geometry powering the visible game. Posts are tagged `[Math]` in the title, with `-math-` in the slug, for filtering. Pitched at high-school / early-uni readers — assume rough familiarity with `cos`/`sin` and don't be scared of an angle, but otherwise don't presume background.

**Convention:** keep at least the next 2–3 posts outlined here so each post can end with a teaser pointing at the next, and so we're always building toward a destination rather than picking ad hoc. When a post ships, drop it from this list and add a new candidate at the end to keep the buffer.

In sequence:

1. **[Math] What a 4×4 matrix actually does to a point** — the view-projection matrix unpacked, one column at a time. Picks up the thread from "the screen is a stack of coordinate systems" (which wrote the matrix-multiply out by hand without explaining it): what each of the 16 numbers contributes, why translation needs the 4th row, and what the perspective divide is really dividing by. The linear-algebra backbone under the projection post.
2. **[Math] Storing a camera as three angles** — spherical coordinates and the orbit camera. Why the viewer keeps yaw/pitch/distance instead of an (x,y,z) eye position, how `dcos`/`dsin` turn three angles into a position on a sphere, the `cos(pitch)` radius-shrink that tucks the camera overhead, and gimbal-ish edge cases at the poles. Teased in the "3D viewer is mostly arithmetic" post (Stop 1).
3. **[Math] From a point on a branch to a dot you can tap** — `branchPoint` gives a world position in viewer units; `projectToScreen` in `src/viewer/projection.ts` turns it into CSS pixels (the view matrix, the perspective divide, the `(ndc + 1) / 2` remap, the y flip, and why a point behind the camera returns nothing). Then why the hit test happens in pixels, not metres: a dot of 8 px radius with a 16 px hit radius is the same size on a phone whatever the zoom, so a far branch is as tappable as a near one. The hotspot overlay as the place where the game's real units and the screen's pixels finally meet.
4. **[Math] A pen line that stays 2 pixels wide** — the ink hull's vertex shader in `src/viewer/TreeViewer.ts` (`OUTLINE_VERTEX`). Each vertex is set back `OUTLINE_DEPTH` (0.1 display units) along the line of sight in view space, projected to clip space, then pushed along its normal's on-screen direction by `uWidth * clip.w`. Why multiplying by `w` is the whole trick: the perspective divide then cancels it, so the push is a fixed amount in normalised device coordinates however far away the vertex is. And why `uWidth` is `2 * OUTLINE_WIDTH_PX / width`: NDC spans 2 units across the viewport, so that is 2 CSS px at any zoom or pixel ratio. A follow-on to item 1's perspective divide, with a working example of using it on purpose.
5. **[Math] Scattering tiles without a random number** — `floorFrame` in `src/world/assets.ts`, `(column * 7 + row * 13) mod frames`, which picks each floor tile's variant and now the shed's wall face and caps too. Why a stable hash of the tile's position beats a random pick (the room looks the same every bake and every reload, nothing flickers or animates, and a test can pin it), and why with 7 and 13 against 3 frames each step of one column or one row changes the frame by 1, so neighbours differ by construction. Then what repeats if the multipliers are chosen badly: a multiplier that is a multiple of the frame count makes whole columns or rows identical, and multipliers equal modulo the frame count give `(column + row) mod frames`, diagonal stripes, which is honestly what 7 and 13 reduce to at 3 frames (both are 1 mod 3), so the post should look at whether the eye catches it.
6. **[Math] A camera that follows you and never shows the void** — `worldView` in `src/world/worldCamera.ts`. On a phone-sized viewport `worldZoom` returns `PHONE_WORLD_ZOOM` (1.25), so the 480 × 270 room-unit canvas shows a 384 × 216 view (`viewSize`: canvas over zoom). `axisStart` then does one axis at a time: centre the view on the player (`focus - size / 2`) and clamp it between the room's drawn extent's start and `start + length - size`, so the camera stops at the walls instead of showing past them. The interesting case is the room shorter than the view: there is no valid clamp interval (its top would be below its bottom), so the view is centred on the extent instead and then kept inside the canvas. Clamping as "the set of allowed positions is an interval, and sometimes it is empty", with the camera-at-a-wall behaviour as the picture.
7. **[Math] A random number you can replay** — the foliage scatter in `src/viewer/foliageMesh.ts`. `addFoliageCluster` draws each leaf's offset from `seededRandom` (mulberry32 in `src/viewer/seededRandom.ts`) as a box, `range(-LEAF_SPREAD, LEAF_SPREAD)` in x and y and half that in z, where `LEAF_SPREAD` is 0.025 m at display scale, so 0.1 units; `foliageSeed` is `tree.id * 1000 + branch.id`, so the same branch gets the same leaves on every rebuild and the jitter fixed in "The leaves wouldn't sit still" cannot come back. The maths: what "pseudo-random" means (a fixed sequence that only looks random, `next()` mapped to `[0, 1)` by dividing by 2^32 and stretched by `range`), uniform in a box versus a sphere (the corners hold leaves a sphere would not, and whether a cluster reads round anyway), when the seed formula collides (branch 1000 of one tree shares a seed with branch 0 of the next), and `leafNormal`, which points each leaf's shading out from the cluster centre and up by `LEAF_NORMAL_LIFT` so crossed quads shade as one rounded mass. A gentle companion to item 5's hash, which picks tiles with no random number at all.
8. **[Math] Which side of a triangle is the front** — `bothWindings` in `src/viewer/toon.ts`, which indexes every triangle of the viewer's meshes twice, `[v, v+1, v+2]` and `[v, v+2, v+1]`. Winding as the sign of a 2D cross product: project a triangle's corners to the screen, take the cross product of two edges, and its sign says whether the corners go round clockwise or anticlockwise, which is all back-face culling looks at. Why drawing both windings under three.js's default `FrontSide` culling (the comment in `TreeViewer.ts` beside the materials) keeps exactly the copy facing the camera, and why that beats a double-sided material, which would flip the stored normal for faces seen from behind (the comment on `bothWindings` says as much), so the post should show what that does to the toon bands on a face seen from its back. Then the tie to "Z-up in a y-down world": the viewer's `(1, -1, 1)` mirror has determinant -1 and reverses every winding on screen, and the doubled index is why that costs nothing. A small, concrete follow-on to the determinant from that post.
9. **[Math] A year in a modulo** — `seasonForDay` in `src/sim/seasons.ts` turns one counter into four seasons with no stored state: `d = max(1, floor(day))`, then `floor(((d - 1) % YEAR_LENGTH) / DAYS_PER_SEASON)` indexes `SEASONS`, where `DAYS_PER_SEASON` is 28 and `YEAR_LENGTH` is 28 × 4 = 112. The `- 1` because the year starts in spring on day 1, the modulo to wrap the year, the integer division to bucket it, and `dayOfSeason` as the same idea one level down. Then `seasonGrowthMultiplier(seasonsActive, season)`: 0 when the season is not in the species' `seasonsActive`, else spring 1.3, summer 1.0, autumn 0.5, winter 0.4. `treeDailyTick` in `src/sim/growth.ts` multiplies it into `growthMult` (with `vitality / 100`, `vigor / 50` and the species' `growthRate`), and that one product scales trunk height, trunk girth, every branch's length and girth, the chance of a new branch and foliage, so a 0 freezes the lot; `fertilizeTree` refuses on the same 0, and `skipTreeTime` runs its ticks without moving the world day, so a skip inside dormancy grows nothing. Summed over a year, the multipliers give "effective growing days": 28 × (1.3 + 1.0 + 0.5) = 78.4 of 112 for the juniper and maple (active spring to autumn), 28 × 3.2 = 89.6 for the pine (all four seasons), and after `growthRate` (1.0, 1.2, 0.7) 78.4, 94.08 and 62.72. Water decay has its own season table (`seasonWaterMultiplier`: 1.0, 1.5, 0.7, 0.3) and could close the post as the counterweight.
10. **[Math] Sixty steps a second on any screen** — the player's walk in `src/world/player.ts` and the clock that drives it in `src/world/input.ts`. `stepPlayer` moves the player `MOVE_SPEED` per step, `fromPrototype(2.5)`, the prototype's 2.5 px over `PROTOTYPE_SCALE` 2, so 1.25 logical px a step and 75 a second at `STEPS_PER_SECOND` 60: a 480-unit-wide canvas crossed in 6.4 seconds. `fixedSteps` turns a display's frame time into whole steps with an accumulator: add the frame's `deltaMs` to the carried remainder, run `floor(total / STEP_MS)` steps (`STEP_MS` = 1000 / 60, about 16.67 ms), carry what is left. The test in `src/world/world.test.ts` pins it at 120 Hz (no step, then one), 30 Hz (two a frame) and 144 frames at 144 Hz (59 or 60 steps), and caps a stall at `MAX_STEPS_PER_FRAME` (5) with the backlog dropped, so a hidden tab does not fast-forward the player. The maths: position as speed times time summed in fixed slices, the same walk as "When the loop disappears" with time in place of arc length, and why a fixed slice makes the walk repeatable where `speed × deltaMs` would not be (the overshoot in "The frame-rate bisect" as the picture). Then the diagonal: input is divided by its length only when that is over 1, so a keyboard diagonal `(1, 1)` moves 1.25 px a step rather than `√2 × 1.25 ≈ 1.77`, while `joystickInput` scales a drag by `1 / max(len, JOYSTICK_RADIUS)` (24 logical px, with a 4 px `JOYSTICK_DEAD_ZONE`) so a short drag walks slower; and each axis is tested against the walls separately, which is why the player slides along a wall instead of sticking to it. A small coda: `WALK_ANIM_STEP` 0.14 a moving step over 4 frames is 8.4 frames and 2.1 walk cycles a second, and `footstepDue` sounds on frames 0 and 2, so 4.2 footsteps a second.
